# A tail inequality for a log-concave density

**The inequality holds.** If a nonnegative random variable has a differentiable log-concave density on a bounded interval, distribution function \(F\), and mean \(\mu\), then

\[
\int_\mu^\infty F(x)\bigl(1-F(x)\bigr)\,dx\ \le\ \mu F(\mu)^2.
\]

The integral stops at the upper endpoint of the support because \(F=1\) there. This answers smcc’s October 2019 question. [Original question](https://math.stackexchange.com/q/3400484)

## Proof

Write \(S=1-F\), \(a=F(\mu)\), and \(s=1-a\). Both \(F\) and \(S\) are log-concave wherever they are positive, a standard consequence of log-concavity of the density. Since \(F(X)\) is uniform on \([0,1]\), Jensen’s inequality for \(\log F\) gives \(\log a\ge\mathbb E[\log F(X)]=-1\). In particular, \(a\ge e^{-1}>1/3\). [Bagnoli and Bergstrom, *Log-concave probability and its applications*](https://escholarship.org/content/qt62c3d5c4/qt62c3d5c4.pdf)

The positive and negative parts of \(X-\mu\) have the same expectation, so

\[
A:=\int_0^\mu F(x)\,dx=\int_\mu^\infty S(x)\,dx.
\]

For \(t\ge0\), midpoint log-concavity gives \(S(\mu+t)^2\ge s S(\mu+2t)\). Integration yields \(\int_\mu^\infty S^2\ge s A/2\). Thus the integral in the question is at most

\[
A-\int_\mu^\infty S(x)^2\,dx\ \le\ \frac{1+a}{2}A. \tag{1}
\]

**If the density is nondecreasing up to \(\mu\)**, then \(F\) is convex on \([0,\mu]\), including the interval below the support where it vanishes. The chord from \((0,0)\) to \((\mu,a)\) gives \(A\le\mu a/2\). Since \(a\ge1/3\), (1) is at most \(\mu a(1+a)/4\le\mu a^2\).

**Otherwise**, unimodality of a log-concave density implies that \(f\) is nonincreasing from \(\mu\) to the upper endpoint. Put \(b=f(\mu)>0\). The tangent line to \(\log F\) at \(\mu\) gives \(F(\mu-t)\le a e^{-bt/a}\) for \(t\ge0\). On the right, \(f\le b\) gives \(S(\mu+t)\ge\max(0,s-bt)\). Integrating both bounds gives

\[
\frac{s^2}{2b}\le A\le\frac{a^2}{b}.
\]

Consequently \(a\ge\sqrt2-1>2/5\). Now put \(H=-\log(1-a)>0\). Log-concavity between \(S(0)=1\) and \(S(\mu)=1-a\) yields \(S(t\mu)\ge e^{-tH}\) for \(0\le t\le1\). Integrating the complementary inequality gives

\[
A\le\mu\left(1-\frac aH\right). \tag{2}
\]

Decomposing \(1/(1-t)=1+t+t^2/(1-t)\) and integrating from \(0\) to \(a\) gives

\[
H\le a+\frac{a^2}{2}+\frac{a^3}{3(1-a)}
  =\frac{a(6-3a-a^2)}{6(1-a)}
  \le\frac{a(1+a)}{(1-a)(1+2a)}.
\]

The final inequality reduces to \(7a+2a^2\ge3\), which holds for \(a\ge2/5\). It follows that \(1-a/H\le 2a^2/(1+a)\). Equations (1) and (2) now give the claimed bound.

## A differentiable counterexample without log-concavity

On \([0,11]\), take the following mixture. With probability \(10^{-4}\), draw uniformly on the interval. With the remaining probability, choose a center \(0.1,1.1,10.1\) with respective probabilities \(0.50,0.45,0.05\), then add a symmetric random perturbation of density proportional to \((0.01^2-t^2)^2\) on \([-0.01,0.01]\). The perturbation density and its derivative both vanish at the ends of its support, so the mixture has a differentiable, positive density on \([0,11]\).

The mean is \(\mu=(1-10^{-4})\,1.05+10^{-4}\,5.5=1.050445\). Only the left bump lies below this mean, giving \(F(\mu)<0.501\) and \(\mu F(\mu)^2<0.265\). Throughout \([1.11,10.09]\), the two left bumps are fully counted and the third contributes zero mass. Hence \(0.949<F(x)<0.951\), so the integral in the question exceeds \(8.98(0.949)(0.049)>0.41\). This reverses the desired inequality.
